Q2  -  WHERE THE OPERATING BOUNDARY ACTUALLY LIES
================================================================================================================
Name: Vignesh S   Register Number: 192325002

WHY THIS PROGRAM EXISTS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PlaylistBenchmark predicted that ArrayList's contiguous shift would beat
  LinkedList's node allocation on SMALL playlists, with LinkedList winning
  only once n grew.  The measurement refuted that: at n = 1,000 ArrayList
  already cost 262 ns per front insert against LinkedList's 59 ns.

  Rather than quietly restating the prediction to match, this program
  finds the real crossover by scanning much smaller sizes.

1.  FRONT INSERTION: SCANNING SMALL n TO FIND THE CROSSOVER
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
n          | ArrayList ns/op  | LinkedList ns/op | ArrayDeque ns/op | WINNER
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8          | 200.87           | 139.70           | 121.75           | LinkedList/Deque
16         | 101.57           | 33.11            | 18.65            | LinkedList/Deque
32         | 102.80           | 16.85            | 13.29            | LinkedList/Deque
64         | 101.41           | 20.37            | 14.60            | LinkedList/Deque
128        | 105.17           | 16.95            | 24.51            | LinkedList/Deque
256        | 115.64           | 31.87            | 22.32            | LinkedList/Deque
512        | 152.81           | 23.05            | 11.26            | LinkedList/Deque
1,024      | 159.05           | 22.12            | 10.55            | LinkedList/Deque
4,096      | 318.09           | 13.56            | 4.50             | LinkedList/Deque

  RESULT: there is NO crossover in the range tested.  ArrayList loses
  front insertion at every size down to n = 8, where the array holds
  eight references and the shift is a single cache line.

  THE PREDICTION WAS WRONG, AND HERE IS WHY.  It assumed the comparison
  was 'copy n references' against 'allocate one node'.  It is not.
  ArrayList.add(0, x) does the arraycopy AND still has to range-check,
  bump modCount and potentially grow the backing array - so it never
  reaches the cheap case the prediction imagined.  LinkedList.addFirst
  writes three references and returns.  The fixed overhead of the array
  path already exceeds a node allocation before the shift is even
  counted, so the shift only widens a gap that was there at n = 8.

  What the prediction got RIGHT is visible two rows down in section 2:
  for MIDDLE insertion, where LinkedList must also walk to the position,
  the contiguous shift does win, and wins by 40x.  The mechanism was
  real; it was applied to the wrong operation.

  Consequence for the recommendation: 'ArrayList is fine for small
  playlists even with front edits' is NOT supported by this evidence and
  is withdrawn.  The defensible statement is narrower - ArrayList is fine
  when front insertion is RARE, whatever the size.
  (first size at which ArrayList lost: n = 8)

2.  WHEN IS ArrayList A REASONABLE CHOICE, AND WHEN WOULD I STOP?
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
USAGE PATTERN                                  | VERDICT        | EVIDENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
append at end, read by index, iterate          | USE IT         | 6.7 ns add, 2.5 ns get, 3.9 ns/elem iterate
playlist under ~500 songs, RARE front edits    | USE IT         | front cost is paid seldom; get/iterate dominate
frequent inserts/removes at the FRONT          | STOP           | 17,968 ns/op at n=100k vs 41 ns for LinkedList
frequent inserts in the MIDDLE                 | STILL USE IT   | 17.8 ms vs LinkedList 723 ms at n=100k
random access by index                         | USE IT         | 2.5 ns vs LinkedList 102,558 ns at n=100k
queue behaviour at both ends                   | STOP           | use ArrayDeque - see section 3

  The row most people get wrong is the MIDDLE insert.  Both structures are
  O(n) there, so the textbook says they are equivalent - but LinkedList
  measured 40x SLOWER at n = 100,000.  Both do O(n) work, yet ArrayList's
  n is a single System.arraycopy of a contiguous block at memory-bandwidth
  speed, while LinkedList's n is n/2 dependent pointer dereferences, each
  a potential cache miss that the CPU cannot prefetch.  Equal complexity
  classes do not imply equal cost.

3.  CHANGED CONDITION: THE 'UP NEXT' QUEUE
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Songs are added at BOTH ends and removed from the FRONT.

DESIGN                     | OPERATION              | SIZE       | MEDIAN (ms)  | PER OP (ns)  | RSD %     | MIN..MAX (ms)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ArrayList                  | up-next mix            | 50,000     | 50.172       | 1003.44      | 3.3       | 46.520..53.402
LinkedList                 | up-next mix            | 50,000     | 1.551        | 31.02        | 9.7       | 1.244..1.827
ArrayDeque                 | up-next mix            | 50,000     | 0.911        | 18.21        | 10.0      | 0.652..1.011

  ArrayDeque is 55x faster than ArrayList and 1.70x faster than LinkedList.

  RECOMMENDATION FOR 'UP NEXT': ArrayDeque.
    - it is a circular array, so adding at either end is O(1) amortised
      with NO shifting: the head and tail indices move instead of the data;
    - it stores elements contiguously, so it keeps ArrayList's cache
      behaviour while gaining LinkedList's cheap ends;
    - it allocates one array rather than one node per element, so it uses
      roughly a third of LinkedList's memory (see section 4).

  WHAT IT COSTS: ArrayDeque has no index-based get(k), so it cannot replace
  the main playlist, where jumping to the k-th song is a core operation.
  The right design is therefore BOTH: ArrayList for the playlist, which is
  read by index and appended to, and ArrayDeque for the Up Next queue,
  which is only ever touched at its ends.  Choosing one structure for the
  whole app is what forces a bad trade; choosing per access-pattern does not.

4.  SPACE PER ELEMENT  (64-bit JVM, compressed references)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STRUCTURE        | PER ELEMENT                              | BYTES          | RELATIVE
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ArrayList        | 1 array slot (reference)                 | 4              | 1.0x
ArrayList (worst) | slot + unused spare capacity (up to 50%) | 4-6            | 1.0-1.5x
ArrayDeque       | 1 array slot, capacity a power of two    | 4-8            | 1.0-2.0x
LinkedList       | Node: header 12 + item 4 + next 4 + prev 4 | 24             | 6.0x

  For a 100,000-song library that is about 400 KB of references against
  2.4 MB of nodes - and the nodes are scattered across the heap, which is
  the underlying reason the pointer-chasing measurements above came out as
  badly as they did.
